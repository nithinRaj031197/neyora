Almost every complaint about fresh mushrooms comes down to storage, and almost
all of it is fixable in thirty seconds.

## The short version

1. **Refrigerate immediately** at 2–4 °C.
2. **Do not wash them** until the minute you cook.
3. **Do not seal them airtight.** Loosely covered, or a paper bag.
4. **Do not freeze them raw.**

## Why airtight fails

Oyster mushrooms are roughly 90% water and they keep respiring after harvest.
Sealed in plastic, that moisture has nowhere to go: it condenses on the caps,
bacteria take the opportunity, and you get a slimy surface within a day.

A paper bag absorbs the excess while still slowing dehydration. It is the single
best thing you can do.

## Where in the fridge

The main compartment or vegetable drawer, never the door — temperature swings
there are worse than the average temperature. Keep them away from anything
strongly aromatic; mushrooms take on smells readily.

## Reading the pack

| Day since harvest | What to expect |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Firm, faint sweet scent, caps squeak when torn. Best. |
| 3–4 | Slightly softer, still excellent cooked hot and fast. |
| 5 | Use today. Best in a fry or a curry rather than a plain sear. |
| 6+ | Check carefully. Slimy patches or a sour smell mean compost. |

## How to tell they have turned

Trust your nose first. Fresh oyster mushrooms smell faintly sweet, a little of
aniseed. A sour or ammonia note means they are done. Wet slimy patches, dark
bruising that spreads, or any visible fuzz — do not cook them.

Dry, slightly leathery edges are not spoilage. They are just dehydrated, and
they will still cook well.

## Freezing

Do not freeze raw — the cell walls rupture and they thaw to mush. Instead sear
them hard in a dry pan first, cool completely, then freeze in a flat layer. They
keep about three months and go straight from frozen into a hot pan.
